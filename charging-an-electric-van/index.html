Charging an electric van: time, cost and home chargers

Updated 5 October 2026. Every figure links to its source. Prices change, so check them before you decide. How we check facts.

Quick answer

  • Overnight at home on a 7kW charger adds about 20 miles of range an hour.
  • On a rapid charger, the vans below go from 10% to 80% in roughly 25–45 minutes.
  • Adding 50kWh costs about £13 at home on a standard tariff and £40 on a public rapid charger.
  • A home charger typically costs £800–£1,200 fitted. Renters and flat owners can get up to £500 off until March 2027.

How long does an electric van take to charge?

It depends on the charger and the van. AC chargers at home or a depot are slower; DC rapid chargers on the public network are much faster.

  • Home (7kW AC): adds about 7kWh an hour. At around 2.8 miles per kWh, that's roughly 20 miles of range for each hour plugged in, so a 71kWh battery takes about 9 hours to go from 10% to full.
  • Depot (11kW or 22kW AC): faster, if the van can accept it. The Ford E-Transit's 22kW AC charger fills it in under six hours. [2]
  • Rapid (DC): the fastest way to top up during a working day.

Rapid charging slows down as the battery fills, which is why times are usually quoted to 80%. Cold batteries charge more slowly too.

Can I charge an electric van at home?

Yes, if you have off-street parking. Most people fit a 7kW wall charger, which typically costs £800–£1,200 supplied and fitted, or more in London and the South East. If your fuse board needs upgrading, that usually adds £300–£800. [9]

  • Grant: the government's EV chargepoint grant pays up to £500 towards a home charger for renters and flat owners. It runs until 31 March 2027, which is its final year. [10]
  • Tariff: an off-peak EV tariff can cut the cost of overnight charging to a fraction of the standard rate (see the table above).
  • A company van at home: agree with your employer how you'll be repaid for the electricity before you start.

Can I charge an electric van without a driveway?

Sometimes. A growing number of councils allow a cross-pavement channel, which lets a cable run safely from your home charger to a van parked on the street. Around 42% of councils in England, Scotland and Wales expect to offer it by the end of 2026, but rules and costs vary: some install the channel free, others charge over £1,000. Ask your council. [11]

Otherwise, you'll rely on public chargers, or on charging at a depot or workplace.

The biggest variable is the electricity supply. A few 22kW AC chargers usually work with the existing supply, but rapid DC chargers often need a grid connection upgrade, which can add £5,000–£40,000. Get a quote from your network operator early. [12] [13]

Most vans spend the night parked, so a few AC chargers usually cover most of a fleet's needs at a fraction of the cost of rapid DC. The grants below reduce the cost further.

What grants are there for depot and workplace charging?

  • Depot Charging Scheme: pays 70% of chargepoint and installation costs for 2026/27 projects, up to £1 million per organisation. The next window opens 28 October 2026 and closes 29 January 2027. Details.
  • Workplace Charging Scheme: £500 per socket, for up to 40 sockets, until 31 March 2027. [10]
  • Tax relief: businesses can deduct the full cost of new chargepoints from profits in the year they buy them (a 100% first-year allowance) until April 2027. Details.

How does public charging work for vans?

  • Price: public charging averaged 54p a kWh on standard chargers and 80p a kWh on rapid and ultra-rapid chargers in July 2026. Prices on the biggest rapid networks ranged from 59p to 92p a kWh. [8]
  • Cost per mile: relying on rapid chargers can cost about as much per mile as diesel, so they work best as a top-up rather than your main way of charging.
  • Space: check the bay before you set off with a long or high-roof van. Not every charging bay is big enough for a large van, and some are under canopies.
